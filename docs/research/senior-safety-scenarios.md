# Senior Safety Scenarios

This document records realistic AskSafe Home safety scenarios for product review, demo preparation, and future rule or test expansion.

The scenarios are written from a senior-first perspective. Each one should help the team answer:

> How does AskSafe reduce uncertainty, loneliness, fear, or decision pressure for seniors?

These are not legal, financial, medical, or emergency advice. They are workflow seeds for helping a user pause, identify what they are being asked to do, and choose a safer next step.

## Scenario 1: Bank Message With A Link

### What Happened

Margaret receives a text message claiming her bank account will be closed unless she taps a link and confirms her details today.

### What They Are Asked To Do

- Click a link
- Give personal details
- Possibly enter banking or card information

### Risk Signals

- The message creates urgency.
- The user is asked to use a link from the message.
- The message asks for account or identity details.
- A real bank should let the user verify through official channels.

### Safer Next Step

Do not tap the link. Open the bank app directly or call the number on the back of the bank card.

### What Not To Do Yet

- Do not tap the message link.
- Do not enter passwords, PINs, one-time codes, or card details.
- Do not reply to the message with personal information.

### How To Verify Safely

Use the bank app, the bank website typed manually, or the phone number printed on the card or statement. If still unsure, ask a trusted person to sit with the user while checking.

## Scenario 2: Urgent Family Money Request

### What Happened

Yvonne gets a message from someone claiming to be her daughter. The message says the daughter has a new phone number, cannot talk, and needs AUD 2,000 urgently.

### What They Are Asked To Do

- Pay money
- Act quickly
- Trust the new contact channel
- Possibly keep the request quiet

### Risk Signals

- The request claims to come from someone close.
- The person says they cannot talk normally.
- The request is urgent and financial.
- The contact channel or number is new.

### Safer Next Step

Pause before paying. Contact the daughter using a number, app account, or contact method that was already trusted before this message arrived.

### What Not To Do Yet

- Do not transfer money yet.
- Do not use bank details provided only in the new message.
- Do not rely on a new number as proof of identity.
- Do not let urgency decide the action.

### How To Verify Safely

Call the saved number, contact another family member, or use a trusted phrase if the family has one. If the person cannot be reached, wait and check again before sending money.

## Scenario 3: Tech Support Call Asking For Device Access

### What Happened

A caller says they are from a technology company and warns that the user's computer or phone has been hacked. They ask the user to install an app and share the screen so they can fix it.

### What They Are Asked To Do

- Install an app
- Share the screen
- Give remote access
- Possibly pay for support

### Risk Signals

- The caller creates fear about a device problem.
- The user is asked to install software during the call.
- The user is asked to share the screen or give remote access.
- The caller may later ask for payment or banking details.

### Safer Next Step

End the call. Do not install anything or share the screen. Contact official support through a trusted website, a known store, or a trusted person.

### What Not To Do Yet

- Do not install remote access tools because a caller asked.
- Do not share the screen.
- Do not open banking while on the call.
- Do not pay for a repair requested by an unexpected caller.

### How To Verify Safely

Use a known support channel, such as the device maker's official website typed manually, a local store, or a trusted family member who can help check the device.

## Scenario 4: Video Call Asking For Money Or Screen Sharing

### What Happened

A person appears on a video call and says they are a relative or trusted contact. The voice and face seem familiar, but they ask for money or ask the user to share the screen to help with an account problem.

### What They Are Asked To Do

- Pay money
- Share the screen
- Trust the face or voice on the call
- Continue under pressure

### Risk Signals

- The request happens on a high-pressure video or chat channel.
- The user is asked for money, account help, screen sharing, or device access.
- The person discourages using another contact method.
- A familiar face or voice may still not be enough to prove identity.

### Safer Next Step

End or pause the call. Contact the person again using a saved number or trusted account. If the family uses a trusted phrase, ask for it before acting.

### What Not To Do Yet

- Do not send money during the call.
- Do not share the screen.
- Do not install apps.
- Do not share passwords, one-time codes, or bank details.

### How To Verify Safely

Use an already saved contact method, call another trusted family member, or ask a pre-agreed trusted phrase. Treat the trusted phrase as one safety step, not final proof that everything is safe.

## Scenario 5: Unexpected Door Knock

### What Happened

Someone knocks at the door and says they are from a utility, charity, or service provider. They ask to come inside, inspect something, or collect payment immediately.

### What They Are Asked To Do

- Open the door
- Let someone inside
- Pay money or share details
- Make a quick decision face to face

### Risk Signals

- The visitor is unexpected.
- The user is pressured to act at the door.
- The visitor asks for payment, personal details, or entry.
- The user may feel socially pressured because the person is physically present.

### Safer Next Step

Keep the door closed. Ask for the company name and reason for the visit. Tell them the user will check independently before deciding.

### What Not To Do Yet

- Do not let the person inside if unsure.
- Do not pay at the door.
- Do not show bills, cards, identity documents, or account details.
- Do not feel rude for taking time.

### How To Verify Safely

Call the company using a number found independently, such as an official bill or website. If the user feels unsafe or threatened, call emergency services.

## Scenario 6: Verification Code Request

### What Happened

A message or caller says they accidentally sent a code to the user's phone and asks the user to read it back. They may say it is needed to recover an account or confirm a payment.

### What They Are Asked To Do

- Share a one-time code
- Share a password or PIN
- Help someone else log in or recover an account

### Risk Signals

- One-time codes are usually meant only for the account owner.
- Sharing a code can let someone access an account.
- The request may sound casual or friendly, but it can still be risky.

### Safer Next Step

Do not share the code. If the user did not request the code, ignore it and check the relevant account directly through the official app or website.

### What Not To Do Yet

- Do not read the code aloud.
- Do not send a screenshot of the code.
- Do not click account recovery links from the message.
- Do not change passwords from a link sent by the requester.

### How To Verify Safely

Open the official app or website directly. If account access looks unusual, change the password through the official channel and ask a trusted person for help if needed.

## Product Notes

These scenarios support the current AskSafe Home flow:

1. The user chooses a situation type.
2. The user describes what happened in their own words.
3. The user identifies what the other person wants them to do.
4. AskSafe shows the safer next step, what not to do yet, why, and how to verify safely.
5. The user may involve trusted support by choice.

The scenarios should not push AskSafe toward surveillance or a generic chatbot. They should keep the product focused on a calm, structured safety decision workflow.
